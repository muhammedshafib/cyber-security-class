# 2. Understand Authentication Mechanisms 🔐

Authentication mechanisms are methods used to verify the identity of a user before allowing them to access a system.

Let's understand each topic in simple words with examples.

## 1. Password-based authentication

Meaning: A user enters a username and password to prove their identity.

Example: You enter your email address and password to log in to Gmail.

How it works:

1. Enter your username.
2. Enter your password.
3. The system verifies your credentials.
4. If they are valid, you can log in.

Security risk: Attackers may steal passwords through phishing, guessing, or data breaches.

Best practice: Use a strong, unique password and a password manager.

## 2. Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

Meaning: MFA requires two or more different types of verification to confirm your identity.

Example of logging into a cloud account:

1. Enter your password.
2. Approve a sign-in request on your phone.
3. Access your account.

The three common authentication factors are:

| Factor             | Example                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Something you know | Password or PIN                 |
| Something you have | Phone or security key           |
| Something you are  | Fingerprint or face recognition |

Why is MFA important? Even if someone steals your password, they may not be able to access your account without the additional factor.

Best practice: Prefer passkeys or phishing-resistant security keys when available.

## 3. Single Sign-On (SSO)

Meaning: SSO allows a user to log in once and access multiple approved applications without entering their credentials separately for each application.

- Email
- HR portal
- Project management application
- Company learning platform

The employee does not need a separate login for every application.

Benefits:

- Saves time.
- Reduces the number of passwords users need to manage.
- Makes account management easier.

Security risk: If the main account is compromised, several connected applications may be at risk. MFA and strong account protection are important.

## 4. Identity federation awareness

Meaning: Identity federation allows a user to access another organization's or service's system using an identity managed by a trusted identity provider.

In simple words, one trusted organization verifies your identity, and another service accepts that verification.

Example: You use your Google account to sign in to another website.

1. You select “Sign in with Google.”
2. Google verifies your identity.
3. Google sends authentication information to the website.
4. The website validates that information and allows access according to its rules.

Common technologies include SAML and OpenID Connect (OIDC).

In cloud security, federation can allow employees to access AWS or Azure using their organization's existing identity system instead of creating separate long-term user accounts for everyone.

Benefits: Easier account management, fewer separate credentials, and centralized identity control.

Important difference: SSO describes the login experience across applications. Federation describes the trust relationship that allows identities to be accepted across systems or organizations. They are related, but not identical.

## 5. Modern authentication practices

Modern authentication uses stronger methods to protect accounts against stolen passwords, phishing, and unauthorized access.

Important practices include:

- Passkeys: Use your device or security key to authenticate, often with a fingerprint, face recognition, or device PIN.
- MFA: Require additional verification for important accounts.
- Least privilege: Give users only the access they need.
- Conditional access: Consider factors such as device security, location, and sign-in risk when deciding whether to allow access.
- Risk-based authentication: Request extra verification when a login looks suspicious.
- Secure sessions: Expire sessions appropriately and revoke access when necessary.
- Monitor login activity: Detect unusual locations, repeated failed logins, and suspicious sign-ins.
- Avoid password sharing: Give every user an individual account.

### Real-world scenario

Suppose an attacker steals an employee's password and tries to log in from an unknown device.

A modern authentication system may:

1. Detect the unusual sign-in.
2. Require MFA or a phishing-resistant authentication method.
3. Block access if the risk is too high.
4. Record the event for security investigation.

This helps protect company data even when a password is compromised.
